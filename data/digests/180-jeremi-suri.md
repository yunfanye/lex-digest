# One-liner

Jeremi Suri uses the American presidency as a lens on the history of power, moving from Washington and Lincoln to Kissinger, the Cold War, foreign policy, institutions, and the difference between commanding authority and statesmanship.

# Summary

Early presidents often had to persuade dispersed institutions and citizens to act, while modern presidents command a much larger executive apparatus capable of direct intervention. Suri emphasizes that durable political power comes from building coalitions, legitimacy, and a shared story that causes others to act without constant coercion.

The conversation examines a foreign-policy style that prioritizes the balance among states and strategic interests, along with the moral costs of treating smaller societies as pieces in that balance. Suri distinguishes the authority attached to a title from the influence of a leader who can frame problems, advise successors, and shape public understanding after leaving power.
