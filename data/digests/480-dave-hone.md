# One-liner

Dave Hone separates the real Tyrannosaurus rex from movie mythology, using biomechanics, fossils, living animals, extinction evidence, and the limits of paleontology to explain what can and cannot be known.

# Summary

Dave Hone starts with the physical oddity of Tyrannosaurus rex: a multi-ton biped carrying an enormous skull, a short muscular neck, and arms that look tiny only beside the rest of the animal. Biomechanical estimates put its highest plausible running speed near twenty-five miles per hour, but hunting did not require movie-style chases. Like modern carnivores, adults probably favored prey smaller or more vulnerable than themselves, while juveniles occupied a faster, lighter ecological niche. Its unusually large size relative to other predators in the same ecosystem made T. rex exceptional even among giant tyrannosaurs.

Paleontology works with a severe sampling problem. Roughly 1,500 to 1,600 valid dinosaur species have been named, with dozens more added each year, yet more than ninety percent of species may be known from a single, often incomplete specimen. Death, burial, mineralization, erosion, discovery, access, and collection each filter the record. Commercial sales such as the $31.8 million T. rex auction can remove important material from public study, and fossils exposed at the surface can literally disappear before scientists obtain the money and permission to excavate them.

The skull and teeth support a bone-crushing bite, but behavior is harder to recover than anatomy. Bite marks can indicate feeding or cannibalism without revealing whether an animal hunted, scavenged, fought, or ate a carcass after death. Brain casts give rough volume, not a clean intelligence score. Sexual dimorphism, display structures, pack hunting, and parenting require population-level evidence that most species lack. Spinosaurus illustrates the danger of confident reconstruction: fragmentary remains, changing limb proportions, and competing interpretations have repeatedly altered claims about its size and swimming ability.

Jurassic Park captured the excitement and broad look of dinosaurs while enlarging Velociraptor, removing feathers, and assigning behaviors that evidence did not establish. Birds are not merely descendants of dinosaurs; they are living dinosaurs, and feathered tyrannosaurs predate the end-Cretaceous extinction by about one hundred million years. The asteroid impact ended a world in which dinosaurs had dominated large-animal niches for over one hundred million years, but one branch survived. New imaging, histology, statistics, and unusually rich fossil beds can still turn sparse bones into better population biology without eliminating uncertainty.

# Takeaways

## T. rex was built for force, not elegance

Its deep skull, thick bones, massive neck, and powerful hind limbs supported a destructive bite and a heavy head. The body plan reflects structural demands rather than movie aesthetics.

## Predators usually avoid equal-sized fights

A failed attack costs energy and can cause fatal injury. Even giant carnivores often choose smaller, younger, isolated, or impaired prey when those options exist.

## One fossil rarely proves a behavior

A tooth mark, brain case, or bone association supports several possible stories. Confidence rises when many specimens preserve age, sex, injury, location, and repeated patterns.

## Private fossils can erase scientific access

High prices encourage discovery but may place specimens beyond repeated measurement, peer review, and future techniques. A fossil's research value depends on long-term accessibility and provenance.

## Jurassic Park mixed evidence with invention

The films made dinosaurs vivid for a generation and funded interest in the field, while their giant featherless raptors and coordinated hunting became misconceptions that outlived the available evidence.

## Birds keep the dinosaur lineage alive

Feathers and birdlike anatomy appear deep within theropod evolution. The asteroid eliminated non-avian dinosaurs, not the entire dinosaur family tree.

## Uncertainty is part of paleontology's result

Sparse samples and destructive geology limit what can be inferred. A responsible reconstruction states which parts follow directly from anatomy and which remain comparative hypotheses.
