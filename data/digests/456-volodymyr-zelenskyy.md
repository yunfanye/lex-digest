# One-liner

Volodymyr Zelenskyy argues that any peace with Russia needs enforceable security guarantees, recounts the failed ceasefires before 2022, and explains Ukraine's positions on NATO, territory, elections, aid, and corruption.

# Summary

Volodymyr Zelenskyy opens by choosing Ukrainian for much of the interview, even though he grew up speaking Russian, because language has become inseparable from identity, invasion, and the claim that Russia acts on behalf of Russian speakers. He discusses family memories of World War II and rejects historical narratives that turn past sacrifice into permission for present conquest. The account culminates in February 24, 2022: intelligence warnings, the first strikes, decisions to remain in Kyiv, and the street video used to show that Ukraine's leadership had not fled.

The peace discussion begins before the full-scale invasion. Zelenskyy recalls the December 2019 Normandy meeting with Vladimir Putin, Emmanuel Macron, and Angela Merkel, prisoner exchanges, ceasefire agreements, and repeated violations. He argues that a new ceasefire without enforcement would give Russia time to rearm. The atrocities discovered in occupied areas hardened public attitudes and narrowed the political space for trust. He remains willing to negotiate, but says Ukraine must enter talks from strength and cannot have its future settled by larger powers without a Ukrainian voice.

NATO membership is presented as the clearest long-term guarantee, though Zelenskyy acknowledges that an interim arrangement may leave some occupied territory outside immediate Ukrainian control. He wants to speak first with Donald Trump, then coordinate with European allies, and uses North Korean participation to argue that the war already extends beyond a bilateral dispute. Legal recognition of annexation is separate from a temporary military reality: his proposed diplomatic path preserves Ukraine's claim to its internationally recognized borders while seeking a sustainable end to fighting.

The final policy sections cover martial law, postponed elections, US aid, corruption, Elon Musk, oligarchs, and postwar reconstruction. Zelenskyy stresses that congressional appropriations do not arrive as a cash transfer of the same amount; much is spent on weapons, logistics, and production in partner countries. He defends elections after martial law and a durable peace make voting possible for soldiers, refugees, and occupied communities. His future Ukraine is digitally administered, less vulnerable to personal connections and oligarchic influence, integrated with Europe, and protected strongly enough for displaced people to return.

# Takeaways

## A ceasefire is not yet peace

Zelenskyy's experience with earlier agreements makes verification, deterrence, and consequences for violations central. A pause without those elements can improve the aggressor's future position.

## Security guarantees must change incentives

Promises matter only if Russia expects a rapid and costly response to renewed attack. Membership, troops, weapons, air defense, and bilateral commitments differ in credibility.

## Territory has military and legal dimensions

A negotiation may freeze control without recognizing annexation. Keeping those concepts separate preserves future diplomacy while acknowledging the battlefield.

## War elections face practical legitimacy tests

Voting during martial law must account for soldiers, refugees, occupied areas, security, media access, and constitutional rules. Delay also needs a clear endpoint and public accountability.

## Aid totals need accounting categories

Appropriated, obligated, delivered, and spent are different numbers. Weapons replacement, US production, transport, humanitarian support, and direct Ukrainian budget aid should be reported separately.

## Digital government can reduce discretion

Automated services and transparent records can limit petty corruption and gatekeeping, while procurement, cybersecurity, surveillance, and high-level political influence still require independent oversight.
