# One-liner

Terence Tao explains how mathematicians attack Navier-Stokes, twin primes, Collatz, and other hard problems, while showing where formal proof systems and AI can extend human experimentation without replacing mathematical judgment.

# Summary

Terence Tao begins with a hard wave problem and moves into the Navier-Stokes regularity question. The equations conserve and move energy across scales, while nonlinear fluid flow can concentrate structure in ways that resist existing estimates. Proving global smoothness requires ruling out every possible singularity; constructing a counterexample requires designing one exact blow-up. Tao describes mathematics as alternating between local technical control and changes in viewpoint that expose a hidden invariant or simpler model.

Conway's Game of Life, infinity, physics, and the nature of reality illustrate the gap between simple rules and intractable behavior. Finite systems can contain more configurations than any computer can enumerate, so mathematicians use symmetry, probability, approximation, and structure rather than brute force. Physical intuition supplies conjectures and examples; proof demands that the conclusion survive all cases allowed by the assumptions, including pathological ones that no experiment is likely to encounter.

AI-assisted theorem proving is already useful through Lean, formal libraries, search, and systems such as DeepMind's AlphaProof. Formalization makes every dependency machine-checkable and allows a changed constant to reveal exactly which later steps fail. Language models can translate ideas, suggest lemmas, run standard techniques, and coordinate large searches. Tao expects research-level human-AI collaborations before autonomous Fields Medal work because choosing important problems, inventing representations, and recognizing explanatory value remain difficult to score.

The final problems make that division concrete. Perelman's proof of the Poincaré conjecture required deep geometric flow and independent judgment; twin-prime progress combined sieve methods, probabilistic models, and large collaboration; Collatz has elementary rules but erratic global behavior; P versus NP asks whether efficiently checking an answer implies efficiently finding one. Tao's advice is to develop several modes of thought, work on problems near the edge of current ability, communicate partial progress, and let tools expand the search without confusing computation with understanding.

# Takeaways

## Hard problems resist one missing trick

Progress often requires many estimates, examples, reductions, and failed approaches before a new viewpoint becomes available. The final proof can hide that exploratory history.

## Proof controls every allowed case

Simulation can build confidence and expose patterns, while a theorem must also handle rare or adversarial configurations that the experiment did not sample.

## Formalization turns prose into dependencies

Lean checks each inference and records which lemmas support the result. This catches silent assumptions and makes collaborative maintenance easier.

## AI is strongest on checkable subproblems

Search, algebra, routine cases, code, and formal verification provide clear feedback. Choosing a fruitful concept or judging a proof's significance has weaker automated rewards.

## Random models guide deterministic questions

Treating primes or dynamical sequences as partly random suggests frequencies and obstructions. The analogy generates conjectures but cannot establish them by itself.

## Mathematical productivity uses several representations

Visual, symbolic, computational, probabilistic, and verbal approaches reveal different structure. Switching representations can be more effective than pushing harder in one language.
