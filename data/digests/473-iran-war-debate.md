# One-liner

Mark Dubowitz and Scott Horton debate whether attacks and maximum pressure can prevent an Iranian bomb or instead destroy inspections, encourage weaponization, widen war, and accelerate nuclear proliferation.

# Summary

Mark Dubowitz of the Foundation for Defense of Democracies and antiwar writer Scott Horton begin from opposite readings of the Israel-Iran conflict and US strikes on nuclear facilities. Dubowitz sees an Iranian regime that accumulated near-weapons-grade uranium, concealed past weapons work, armed regional proxies, and used negotiations to preserve a path to a bomb. Horton sees decades of threat inflation, covert attacks, sanctions, and US withdrawal from diplomacy pushing Iran from a monitored civilian program toward higher enrichment and confrontation.

The technical dispute separates uranium enrichment from a finished weapon. Iran's stock of 60% enriched material shortens the time required to reach roughly 90%, but weaponization also involves conversion, metallurgy, explosive design, delivery, command, and a political decision. The 2007 US National Intelligence Estimate assessed that an organized weapons program had halted in 2003; the later Iranian nuclear archive supplies evidence of prior work and, for Dubowitz, intent that never disappeared. The speakers disagree over how much confidence to place in intelligence agencies, IAEA access, and documents obtained by Israel.

The JCPOA is the main policy counterfactual. Horton argues that the agreement capped enrichment, reduced stockpiles, and installed inspections until the United States left in 2018, after which Iran expanded its program. Dubowitz argues that sunset clauses, advanced centrifuge research, missiles, undeclared sites, and regional activity made the deal a temporary route to a more capable threshold state. Their disagreement is partly over the feasible alternative: a stronger negotiated agreement, indefinite pressure, regime change, or military enforcement each assumes different Iranian responses.

Best- and worst-case scenarios include a delayed program, hidden surviving facilities, Iranian withdrawal from the Non-Proliferation Treaty, a sprint to a weapon, attacks on US forces, regional war, regime collapse, and proliferation by Saudi Arabia, Turkey, Egypt, or others. The closing sections on libertarianism, FDD, Trump, World War II, and World War III expose competing lessons from history. Dubowitz emphasizes the cost of appeasing aggressive regimes; Horton emphasizes wars launched by exaggerated certainty. The debate is most useful when each factual claim is separated from its strategic inference.

# Takeaways

## Enrichment is not a complete weapon

A short breakout estimate describes production of weapons-grade material. It does not by itself establish a tested device, reliable delivery system, or political order to build one.

## Inspections create both access and dependency

Monitoring can reveal stockpiles and facilities, while an attack or treaty withdrawal can remove inspectors and increase uncertainty exactly when risk is highest.

## A strike has several success criteria

Damage to buildings, loss of equipment, scientist attrition, delay in enrichment, and change in political intent are different outcomes. A tactical hit may fail strategically.

## Sanctions can coerce or entrench

Economic pressure may force concessions, strengthen hardliners, expand smuggling networks, punish civilians, or persuade leaders that only a nuclear deterrent ensures survival.

## Historical analogies select different errors

Munich warns against appeasement; Iraq warns against threat inflation; Libya warns regimes about disarmament. Policy depends on which mechanism actually matches Iran.

## Proliferation responds to regional credibility

Neighbors watch Iran, Israel, and US guarantees. If diplomacy or protection appears unreliable, more states may seek independent nuclear options.
