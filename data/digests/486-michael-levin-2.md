# One-liner

Michael Levin argues that minds exist across biological and artificial scales, and that experiments on persuadability, morphogenesis, memory, and collective behavior can reveal intelligence where familiar categories hide it.

# Summary

Michael Levin proposes a practical test for intelligence: vary how a system is addressed, then measure which interventions change its behavior. He calls the result a spectrum of persuadability, ranging from direct physical control through rewards, signals, and reasoning. His related idea of a cognitive light cone describes the spatial and temporal scale of the goals a system can pursue. A bacterium manages a tiny local future, while a human can care about markets, descendants, and events far beyond the body. The category matters less than the experimental response.

That approach leads Levin to distrust hard boundaries such as living versus non-living, cognitive versus mechanical, and software versus hardware. He argues that such labels discourage researchers from transferring useful tools across fields. In development, cells can reach a target anatomy through unfamiliar routes after barriers or injuries disrupt the normal process. Levin treats this compensatory behavior as evidence for goal-directed control, while warning that a visible outcome alone does not prove a goal. The test is whether the system corrects deviations under new conditions.

The conversation extends the same reasoning to memory, algorithms, and perception. Levin discusses experiments in which learned associations can survive a caterpillar's metamorphosis, suggesting that memory may not reside in one fixed anatomical substrate. He describes brains as interfaces to patterns that may not be exhausted by their physical implementation. Sorting algorithms provide a deliberately austere case: deterministic code can still produce collective behaviors that were not part of the programmer's usual description, offering a clean laboratory for studying how observers recognize agency.

Levin connects the theory to medicine. His lab tries to communicate with tissues through bioelectric and chemical signals so cells adopt a new anatomical target, rather than micromanaging every molecular step. He reports that anthrobots made from adult human cells measured roughly twenty percent younger than their source cells, a preliminary result rather than a general reversal of aging. He also argues that any serious account of mind uploading must include the distributed relationships among cells, organs, stress signals, and the environment. The closing advice is to protect a private space for strange ideas while subjecting claims about unfamiliar minds to experiments.

# Takeaways

## Persuadability can be measured

Instead of deciding in advance whether a system is intelligent, try physical interventions, training, rewards, signals, and reasons. The cheapest effective method reveals where the system sits on a behavioral control spectrum.

## Goals have a scale

A cognitive light cone describes how far through space and time a system's concerns extend. This gives researchers a common language for comparing bacteria, tissues, animals, people, and engineered agents.

## Categories can block useful experiments

Calling one system cognitive and another mechanical can prevent behavioral tools from being tried in the second domain. Levin prefers provisional categories that do not restrict the experimental toolbox.

## Correction is evidence of a target

A developmental system shows more than open-loop execution when it finds an alternate route after its normal path is obstructed. Repeated compensation under novel perturbations supports a goal-directed model.

## Memory may outlive its substrate

Learned associations surviving metamorphosis challenge simple stories in which memory is tied to a fixed collection of neurons. The result points toward information stored across changing biological organization.

## Regeneration is a communication problem

Levin's group tries to alter the target state that tissues pursue through bioelectric and biochemical cues. The cells perform much of the detailed construction once they accept a different anatomical goal.

## Alien minds may already surround us

Cells navigate high-dimensional physiological and anatomical spaces that human intuition rarely notices. Searching for unfamiliar intelligence therefore includes learning to see problem-solving inside ordinary bodies and algorithms.
