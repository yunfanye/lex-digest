# One-liner

Jeff Kaplan recounts the player obsession that led him to Blizzard, the design cultures behind World of Warcraft and Overwatch, the failure of Titan, his departure, and his return to small-team game making.

# Summary

Jeff Kaplan's route into games ran through literature, low-paid writing work, and an all-consuming EverQuest guild. As a raid leader he learned to coordinate dozens of players, write strategy posts, manage conflict, and understand why shared adversity creates durable stories. Several guildmates secretly worked at Blizzard. Informal lunches with them became an interview because Kaplan spoke as a player rather than as an applicant; an expensive teleport potion he had once given a stranger in EverQuest turned out to have saved Blizzard co-founder Allen Adham's character.

Early Blizzard was small, confident, and willing to work from direct play experience. The World of Warcraft team borrowed the persistent world and social scale of EverQuest while removing needless punishment, improving quest guidance, and making the environment readable to a broader audience. Kaplan argues that games produce their best narratives through players' actions, relationships, failures, and discoveries. Written story can support that space, but it becomes a problem when it interrupts play or assumes the author is more interesting than the player's own experience.

Titan supplied the opposite lesson. Blizzard assembled experienced people to build the next revolutionary MMO, expanded the technology and class design, and spent years on a concept Kaplan believed could not ship. He repeatedly asked leadership to stop the project rather than keep burning money. After Titan closed, a small group used its art, ideas, and hard lessons to prototype Overwatch with a narrower goal: distinct heroes, a few legible abilities, quick team play, and a world that could grow after the first game. Kaplan summarizes the production correction as crawl, walk, run rather than trying to begin with the next World of Warcraft.

Success introduced other pressures: matchmaking, toxicity, esports, free-to-play economics, content cadence, executive targets, and a growing gap between the game team and corporate control. Kaplan says that loss of control, more than one isolated decision, broke his belief that he would retire at Blizzard. Away from the company, learning Unreal, Blender, and Illustrator alone restored the pleasure of making things. His new project, The Legend of California, combines open-world survival and multiplayer adventure in the Gold Rush era. Its planned resets borrow from Rust: a wipe can be exciting when it lets veterans and newcomers begin the next story together.

# Takeaways

## Player experience can be a design résumé

Kaplan's guild leadership demonstrated systems thinking, communication, encounter analysis, and community judgment. Blizzard recognized skills that a conventional writing résumé did not show.

## The best game stories may be lived

A rescue, betrayal, raid failure, or lucky discovery gains meaning because a player caused it with other people. Authored narrative works best when it creates conditions for those memories rather than replacing them.

## Accessibility need not remove depth

World of Warcraft reduced friction that did not create interesting decisions while keeping a large world, class identity, social dependence, and difficult group content.

## Expertise can inflate ambition

Titan's team knew how important World of Warcraft had been and tried to design another phenomenon from the start. That burden encouraged scale and complexity before the core game had proved itself.

## A failed project can fund the next design

Overwatch reused talent, visual work, technology, and negative lessons from Titan. The value came from selecting a smaller playable idea, not pretending the lost years had produced nothing.

## Live-service design is organizational design

Matchmaking, updates, esports, monetization, moderation, and staffing shape the game alongside maps and heroes. When business structures change, creative authority and player trust change with them.

## Resets can restore shared beginnings

Persistent progression separates a veteran from a new player. A well-designed wipe removes accumulated advantage and makes the opening scramble itself part of the recurring game.
