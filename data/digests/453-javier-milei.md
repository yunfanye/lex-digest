# One-liner

Argentina's president Javier Milei defends fiscal shock therapy, deregulation, and anarcho-capitalist ideals, presenting his first year as a fight against inflation, political privilege, and decades of state expansion.

# Summary

Javier Milei explains his conversion to Austrian economics through long-run growth data and writers such as Murray Rothbard and Alberto Benegas Lynch. He identifies capitalism, private property, and voluntary exchange as the source of the post-1800 rise in living standards, and describes anarcho-capitalism as his moral ideal even while governing through the institutions of a nation-state. His practical program aims to remove regulations, shrink ministries, reduce public employment, open markets, and eventually complete thousands of additional structural reforms.

The economic case centers on the crisis he says he inherited in December 2023: daily inflation near 1%, wholesale inflation of 54% for the month, a long record of fiscal deficits, central-bank liabilities, price controls, and a fall in GDP per capita. Milei describes an immediate fiscal adjustment, monetary restraint, deregulation through Decree 70/2023, the Bases Law, subsidy changes, and layoffs or nonrenewal of public workers. He credits these measures with a fiscal surplus, lower monthly inflation, falling country risk, and improved relative prices, while acknowledging that the initial contraction and poverty increase imposed severe costs.

Milei presents corruption as a network of political privileges, intermediaries, subsidized organizations, protected businesses, and media incentives rather than only illegal payments. He argues that reducing the state removes opportunities for extraction and makes reform politically threatening to those who benefit from the old system. His rhetoric treats the conflict as a global struggle between freedom and socialism, which informs his affinity for Elon Musk, Donald Trump, and the proposed US Department of Government Efficiency.

The latter half broadens from US-Argentina relations and dollarization to football, religion, rock music, markets, loyalty, and personal endurance. Milei compares Messi and Maradona, calls Elvis the greatest rock singer, and describes faith as support against daily attacks. His defense of markets is moral as well as technical: profit follows from serving customers with better goods at lower prices. The episode records the government's own account during an early and volatile stage; its claims require comparison with later inflation, wages, poverty, output, and institutional durability.

# Takeaways

## Fiscal balance was the first constraint

Milei treats elimination of the deficit as the prerequisite for stopping monetary financing and restoring credibility. Other reforms are organized around preserving that constraint.

## Shock therapy front-loads social costs

Removing subsidies, correcting controlled prices, and cutting public spending can improve fiscal accounts while reducing real income and activity in the near term. Evaluation needs both stabilization and distributional measures.

## Deregulation redistributes political power

Each rule or subsidy has beneficiaries, administrators, and dependent businesses. Removing it changes income and influence, which explains why technical reforms become political battles.

## An ideal still needs transition institutions

Anarcho-capitalist principles do not specify how contracts, currency, safety nets, competition, and public services move from the existing system without disruption or capture.

## Inflation measures tell different stories

Monthly, annualized, wholesale, and consumer inflation can move at different speeds. Claims of stabilization should state the index, comparison period, and effect on wages and purchasing power.

## Early indicators are provisional evidence

Fiscal surplus and falling country risk can signal improved confidence, while durable success also requires investment, employment, poverty reduction, and rules that survive beyond one leader.
