# One-liner

Cuneiform scholar Irving Finkel explains how writing emerged, how lost scripts are deciphered, what Mesopotamian tablets say about gods and floods, and why an ancient board game traveled for millennia.

# Summary

Irving Finkel begins with the oldest surviving evidence of writing, clay tablets from Mesopotamia around 3500 BC, while stressing that the first surviving example need not mark the invention itself. Early signs were pictorial and administrative, but cuneiform grew into a system whose signs could represent objects, words, syllables, and several different sounds. That multivalence made the script powerful and difficult. Sumerian, Akkadian, and neighboring languages could use the same wedge-shaped technology, much as different modern languages use related alphabets.

Decipherment depended on patient comparison rather than one spectacular guess. Old Persian royal inscriptions supplied repeated names and multilingual parallels; scholars then learned that a single sign could carry several values. Finkel describes the practical craft of reading damaged tablets: identify the language, compare formulaic passages, consult dictionaries built over generations, inspect the physical traces, and remain willing to leave a gap. He also advances a controversial inference that a complex site such as Göbekli Tepe may have required records, while acknowledging that no writing has been found there.

The tablets preserve a world of gods, ghosts, legal disputes, medicine, jokes, school exercises, and literature. The flood story entered modern scholarship when George Smith recognized a Nineveh tablet parallel to Genesis in 1872. Mesopotamian versions in Atrahasis and Gilgamesh are older than the surviving biblical text and share a divinely ordered flood, a vessel, saved life, birds, and survival. Finkel thinks the tradition may preserve the memory of a real regional catastrophe, but the tablets show centuries of literary transmission rather than a geological proof of one global flood.

Finkel's reconstruction of the Royal Game of Ur illustrates what material culture can reveal when text and objects meet. Boards appear from roughly 2600 BC through the first millennium and across Mesopotamia, Egypt, the Levant, Anatolia, Greece, and Crete. A rules tablet let him restore a playable race game, though some details remain interpretive. He closes with the British Museum's role as a storehouse for shared human evidence and with a refusal to romanticize antiquity: ancient people lied, loved, fought, cheated, worshiped, and played much as people do now.

# Takeaways

## Survival dates are not invention dates

The earliest known tablets date to about 3500 BC, but clay preservation only establishes a lower bound. Writing may have developed through earlier media or stages that left no recoverable trace.

## Cuneiform signs carried several jobs

One sign could denote a thing, a word, a syllable, or several sounds. Readers resolved the intended value through language, grammar, position, and context.

## Decipherment is cumulative

Proper names, repeated formulas, bilingual inscriptions, sign lists, and dictionaries gradually constrain a script. A convincing reading must keep working across many independent texts.

## Complexity does not prove writing

Finkel suspects Göbekli Tepe's organization required records, but no inscription establishes the claim. The distinction between a plausible administrative need and archaeological evidence matters.

## Flood stories have a textual history

Atrahasis, Gilgamesh, and Genesis share narrative elements, yet each belongs to a different literary setting. Their relationship supports transmission and adaptation more directly than a single literal reconstruction.

## Games can map cultural exchange

Royal Game of Ur boards appeared over a vast region for nearly two thousand years. Their distribution records trade, imitation, leisure, and contact that political chronicles often omit.

## Ancient people were recognizably human

Tablets preserve affection, fraud, ambition, fear, humor, and bureaucracy. Finkel argues that studying these ordinary motives prevents antiquity from becoming either primitive caricature or moral fantasy.
