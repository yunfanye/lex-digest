# One-liner

Astrophysicist Adam Frank connects exoplanets, planetary evolution, technosignatures, and the physics of life to a disciplined search for alien civilizations and a broader critique of reductionist science.

# Summary

Adam Frank begins with planet formation and the discovery that worlds are a normal outcome of star formation. The first confirmed exoplanets and the 1995 detection of a planet around a Sun-like star changed the Drake equation from speculation about planets to an observational science. He explains habitable zones, plate tectonics, atmospheric evolution, extinction, and the coevolution of a biosphere with its planet. Human technology now forms a technosphere whose energy use, chemistry, and waste can alter Earth on a geological scale.

The Fermi paradox asks why a galaxy old enough for repeated technological civilizations appears silent. Frank argues that the usual formulation often assumes unlimited growth, indefinite survival, and rapid colonization. Models with finite civilizational lifetimes produce a changing galactic population rather than a permanent empire. Using the number of habitable-zone planets, his work estimates that humanity would be unique in cosmic history only if the probability of a technological species were below roughly one in ten billion trillion.

The practical search covers radio signals, atmospheric pollutants, city lights, solar panels, industrial chemistry, waste heat, spacecraft plumes, and large structures such as Dyson spheres. New telescopes and machine-learning methods could detect some of these technosignatures across interstellar distances. Mars and nearby worlds provide nearer tests of life's prevalence, while interstellar travel remains constrained by energy, time, radiation, and the uncertain feasibility of generation ships, cryogenic preservation, or warp-drive concepts.

Frank is skeptical that current UFO reports establish extraterrestrial visitation because the evidence lacks the clarity expected from repeated contact by advanced craft. The final sections move from astrobiology into a philosophy of science developed with Marcelo Gleiser and Evan Thompson. Experience, embodiment, agency, and time are treated as parts of nature that scientific practice cannot simply erase from its own account. His Zen practice informs a closing reflection on mortality: direct experience can loosen the feeling of a separate self without supplying a conventional claim about an afterlife.

# Takeaways

## Exoplanets changed the base rate

Planets are abundant enough that the main uncertainty has shifted toward the origins of life, intelligence, and technological longevity. Astronomy can now constrain parts of the Drake equation with data.

## Civilizations may be temporally sparse

A galaxy can host many technological species across billions of years while containing few at the same moment. Finite lifetimes weaken the intuition that old civilizations must fill every system.

## Technology leaves planetary fingerprints

Industrial gases, heat, lights, materials, and energy collection can alter a planet's spectrum. Searching for these effects broadens SETI beyond deliberate radio messages.

## Mars colonization is an engineering system

Transport is only one layer. Radiation, food, ecology, maintenance, medicine, reproduction, governance, and economic purpose determine whether a settlement can persist.

## UFO claims need sensor-grade evidence

Ambiguous videos and testimony justify investigation, but extraterrestrial conclusions require repeatable measurements, multiple independent sensors, physical traces, and exclusion of ordinary causes.

## Science includes the knowing organism

Measurements, models, and shared validation are activities performed by embodied agents. A complete account of nature must eventually explain experience and cognition rather than treating them as errors outside physics.
