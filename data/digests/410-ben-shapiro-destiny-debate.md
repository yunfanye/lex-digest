# Takeaways

## Name the crux

Many factual disputes are downstream of different priorities: stability versus reform, outcomes versus procedure, or freedom from government versus social coercion. Naming the crux prevents endless trading of examples.

## Political risk depends on a baseline

A voter who sees institutions as resilient may focus on policy; one who sees a candidate as a unique threat may prioritize the transfer of power. The baseline often explains the conclusion.

## Institutional criticism needs a replacement model

Claims that universities, media, or government are captured should specify who decides instead, what accountability applies, and how errors are corrected.

## Foreign-policy analogies compress detail

Munich, Iraq, and Cold War analogies can illuminate one mechanism while hiding geography, capability, alliance commitments, and local agency.

## Civil tone improves diagnosis, not truth

Extended answers and direct rebuttal make assumptions easier to hear. Confidence and fluency still do not establish disputed historical or empirical claims.

## Personal norms reveal theories of human behavior

The relationship discussion exposes beliefs about jealousy, commitment, convention, experimentation, and whether institutions encode wisdom or outdated constraint.
