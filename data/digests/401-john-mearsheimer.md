# One-liner

John Mearsheimer applies offensive realism to Russia-Ukraine, Israel-Palestine, China, and great-power rivalry, offering a coherent account of how states pursue security while illustrating the moral and empirical limits of treating power as the dominant cause.

# Summary

John Mearsheimer is a University of Chicago political scientist associated with offensive realism. His framework begins with an anarchic international system: no world government reliably protects states, intentions are uncertain, and survival encourages powerful states to seek regional dominance. The episode applies that lens to Hitler, NATO, Ukraine, Israel and Palestine, China, and nuclear risk.

Mearsheimer argues that Western policy and NATO expansion played a central role in producing the Ukraine crisis, while Russia bears responsibility for choosing invasion. On Israel-Palestine, he emphasizes occupation, power asymmetry, U.S. support, and the shrinking feasibility of two states. These interpretations are influential and highly contested; critics argue that he underweights ideology, domestic politics, smaller-state agency, imperial ambition, and evidence that leaders sometimes accept severe costs for goals not reducible to security.

The China section follows the same logic: a rising China will seek greater regional control, the United States will try to prevent a peer competitor, and security competition can intensify even when neither side wants war. This is a useful baseline model because it forces attention to capability and incentives. It becomes misleading when presented as destiny rather than one mechanism among several.

Realism states its assumptions clearly enough to test. Its explanatory reach depends on which facts it explains, which it demotes, and what observation would force an update. Comparing those answers with rival historical and political accounts exposes both the theory's power and its limits.

# Takeaways

## Security competition can emerge without evil intent

When states cannot know future intentions, one state's defensive buildup can look offensive to another. This security dilemma helps explain arms races but does not absolve leaders of choices or legal responsibility.

## A theory's strength is also its blind spot

Realism highlights power, geography, and survival with unusual clarity. The same focus can discount identity, law, ideology, leaders, public opinion, and the agency of countries located between great powers.

## Causal explanation is not moral justification

Explaining why an invasion became more likely does not make it permissible. Analysts should keep prediction, responsibility, and preferred policy in separate columns.

## Historical analogies need mechanism, not resemblance

Hitler, appeasement, the Cold War, and imperial precedents can illuminate a specific dynamic. They become propaganda when surface similarity substitutes for evidence about capability, aims, and constraints.

## Small states are actors, not terrain

Great-power models often describe Ukraine, Palestinians, Israelis, or Taiwan through the interests of larger states. Their own political choices, fears, institutions, and rights remain causally and morally relevant.

## The best use of realism is stress-testing

Ask how a proposed policy changes an adversary's incentives, alliance credibility, and escalation risk. Then combine that answer with law, ethics, domestic legitimacy, and the lived consequences for civilians.
