# One-liner

Michael Mina argues for an engineering approach to pandemic control built around frequent rapid testing, large-scale surveillance, and systems that measure infectiousness quickly enough to change behavior before transmission chains grow.

# Summary

Mina distinguishes tests designed to diagnose an individual patient from frequent screening tests whose value comes from quickly identifying people likely to be infectious. A cheaper test used repeatedly may catch contagious periods more reliably than a highly sensitive laboratory test whose result arrives after the person has already exposed others.

Mina imagines combining population sampling, rapid diagnostics, and data infrastructure so outbreaks become visible as changing regional patterns rather than surprises discovered after hospitals fill. The conversation emphasizes manufacturing, regulation, distribution, incentives, communication, and user behavior alongside the underlying assay technology.
