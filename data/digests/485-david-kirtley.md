# One-liner

David Kirtley explains fusion physics and Helion's pulsed field-reversed reactors, including direct electricity recovery, safety, a 2028 Microsoft target, and the engineering gaps between plasma experiments and commercial power.

# Summary

David Kirtley contrasts fission, which splits heavy nuclei such as uranium, with fusion, which combines light isotopes and converts a small mass difference into energy. Fusion fuel must be heated beyond one hundred million degrees so nuclei can overcome electrical repulsion, then confined at sufficient density for sufficient time. The temperature describes particle speed rather than a solid object glowing at that temperature. The difficulty of maintaining those conditions is also a safety feature: unlike a fission chain reaction, fusion stops when heating and confinement fail.

Helion pursues a pulsed approach based on field-reversed configurations, compact plasma structures held by their own currents and external magnetic fields. Two plasmas are formed, accelerated toward one another, merged, and rapidly compressed by magnetic fields. The system can involve currents on the order of one hundred million amps for brief periods. Modern power semiconductors, switching, diagnostics, fiber optics, simulation, and control allow a version of theta-pinch physics explored in the 1950s to be driven with timing and repeatability unavailable to the original experiments.

The fuel strategy emphasizes deuterium and helium-3. Instead of sending all fusion heat through a blanket, boiler, steam turbine, and cooling tower, Helion aims to recover changing magnetic energy directly as electricity and recirculate much of the input energy. Kirtley cites high component-level recovery efficiencies and argues that direct conversion could compensate for a harder fuel cycle. The full plant still has to demonstrate net electricity, component lifetime, fuel supply, repetition rate, maintenance, radiation management, and reliable operation across millions of pulses.

Helion signed an agreement in 2023 to target electricity for a Microsoft data center in 2028. Kirtley repeatedly calls the schedule ambitious; it is a commercial target, not a completed demonstration. The proposed architecture uses modular plants of roughly fifty megawatts, which could cluster near data centers and adjust output through pulse frequency. If it works economically, widely available fuel and compact generation could reduce dependence on pipelines and fuel-exporting states, support rising AI electricity demand, and expand the energy budget available to civilization. The episode's optimism rests on a clear remaining test: turning controlled fusion shots into dependable grid power.

# Takeaways

## Fusion fails off rather than running away

The plasma requires continuous, precise heating and confinement. A disturbance cools or disperses the fuel, ending the reaction instead of sustaining a chain process.

## One hundred million degrees describes motion

In a plasma, temperature measures the kinetic distribution of particles. The plasma is extremely hot but low in mass and separated from ordinary material by fields and vacuum.

## Helion compresses plasma in pulses

Short, intense magnetic compression can reach fields and power density beyond steady-state magnets. Commercial use then demands high repetition and long component life.

## Direct recovery changes the plant equation

Recapturing magnetic energy as electricity could avoid the efficiency loss and large thermal equipment of a steam cycle. Whole-system efficiency matters more than an isolated component result.

## Advanced fuel trades neutrons for temperature

Deuterium-helium-3 reactions can reduce neutron output compared with common deuterium-tritium designs, but require hotter conditions and still produce secondary reactions that engineers must manage.

## The 2028 date is a target

A power-purchase agreement creates focus and a customer, but it does not prove the physics, reliability, regulatory path, or delivery schedule in advance.

## Data centers fit modular generation

Large computing campuses need dense, continuous electricity and can add repeated units at one site. They may become early customers before fusion competes across an entire grid.
