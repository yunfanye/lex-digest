# One-liner

Historian Anthony Kaldellis argues the "Byzantine Empire" was in fact the unbroken Roman Empire for 2,200 years, and unpacks the institutions, taxation, and consensual politics that let it survive—and why it ultimately fell.

# Summary

Historian Anthony Kaldellis (Ohio State) joins Lex Fridman for a chronological tour of Roman history from the founding of Rome in 753 BC through the fall of Constantinople in 1453 AD. His central claim is definitional: what historians call the "Byzantine Empire" never existed as a separate entity. It was the direct, unbroken continuation of the Roman state, whose subjects called themselves Romans throughout, spoke of their polity as "Romania," and maintained Roman law, citizenship, and identity for another thousand years after Rome's western half collapsed in 476 AD. The label "Byzantine" was invented by later Western European historians for political reasons, to deny cultural kinship with a Greek-speaking, Orthodox Christian society.

The conversation traces major turning points with care to distinguish sudden decisions (Constantine's conversion to Christianity, Caracalla's 212 AD edict granting citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants) from long, gradual processes (the East-West split, the fall of Rome, the slow Christianization of the empire, which took roughly five centuries to reach a solid majority). Kaldellis details the Crisis of the Third Century (26 emperors murdered in 50 years, hyperinflation, plague, invasion) and how Diocletian's reforms—universal taxation, a larger bureaucracy, the Tetrarchy—stabilized the empire and created the administrative template that lasted centuries. He walks through Constantine's founding of Constantinople as a deliberate "New Rome," his brutal family murders alongside the decisions that reshaped the empire, and argues Constantine's Christian conversion was likely sincere rather than cynically political, since Christians were a small, not particularly powerful minority (perhaps 10%) at the time.

Kaldellis argues the empire functioned as a "monarchic republic": an emperor sitting atop a republican political culture, where legitimacy was constantly tested through public acclamation in venues like the Hippodrome, petitions, and the ever-present threat of civil war or palace coup (46% of Constantinople's emperors were violently overthrown). This pushed emperors to govern in their subjects' interest rather than merely claim to, and the empire almost never used its army for internal social control, a rarity in world history. He discusses Justinian's mixed legacy (legal codification via the Corpus Juris Civilis, reconquest of the West, Hagia Sophia, but also overextension and the brutal suppression of the Nika riots), the near-collapse and recovery from the 7th-century Arab conquests (aided by Greek fire), the 8th-century Arab siege's failure under Leo III, and the 11th-century "triple whammy" of Normans, Pechenegs, and Seljuk Turks that Kaldellis attributes primarily to external shocks rather than internal decay, a direct challenge to the traditional "decline and fall" narrative.

The episode closes on Kaldellis's broader framework: the empire's history is best understood through the centuries of steady, self-generated stabilization and regrowth that followed each crisis, until the 1300s-1450s, when Ottoman Turkish expansion finally proved unrecoverable. He credits the empire's exceptional longevity to a persuasive and largely sincere governing rhetoric matched by action, plus a tightly fused Roman-Orthodox identity that gave subjects strong reasons to stay unified rather than splinter. The conversation ends with reflections on modern parallels (the gap between American rhetoric and action in foreign policy), human nature's constancy across history, and cautious optimism about humanity's current technological and moral trajectory.

# Takeaways

## The 'Byzantine Empire' is a historiographical myth

The Eastern Roman Empire was never called that by its own people; they called themselves Romans and their state Romania throughout its 1,000+ year existence after Rome's western half fell. The term 'Byzantine' was coined by later Western European historians for political reasons to deny continuity with Rome.

## The Edict of Caracalla (212 AD) was a genuine, enforced revolution

Caracalla's Constitutio Antoniniana extended Roman citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the empire, and unlike similar gestures in other empires, it had real teeth: within a generation, all emperors and the most powerful officials came from the provinces, not Rome itself.

## Civil war and the threat of violent overthrow functioned as a check on power

With no hereditary right to the throne and roughly 46% of Constantinople's emperors overthrown by violence, emperors had to govern well and responsively rather than rule as unchecked tyrants; civil war operated like a 'perpetual referendum.'

## Public assemblies like the Hippodrome functioned as a form of ancient democratic feedback

Crowds of up to 100,000 people gathered to cheer or jeer emperors; policies (like a proposed 'German tax' under Alexius III) could be reversed on the spot based on public reaction, giving subjects real leverage over imperial decisions.

## The empire almost never used its army for internal social control

Unlike most historical military-backed regimes, Roman/Byzantine emperors rarely deployed the army to suppress their own population, largely because the army was recruited from and embedded in local communities and seen as protecting rather than policing subjects.

## Taxation, not religion or ideology, was the primary glue holding the empire together

A dense, centuries-long institutional matrix of census-taking, tax collection, coinage, and legal courts reached even the most remote villages, meaning there were no truly 'isolated' communities disconnected from the state.

## The empire's long-term decline was driven by external shocks, not internal decay

Kaldellis argues against the traditional 'decline and fall' narrative: the empire repeatedly recovered from major territorial losses through internally generated stability and regrowth, only failing to recover from the sequential 14th-century blows of losing Asia Minor, civil wars, Serbian expansion, and the Black Death.

## Constantine's Christian conversion was likely a genuine personal belief, not political calculation

Christians were a small, not particularly powerful minority (perhaps 10%) at the time of Constantine's conversion, undermining cynical political explanations; most scholars now believe he genuinely believed the Christian god had helped him in battle.

## Sincere, credible rhetoric mattered as much as action in sustaining the empire's legitimacy

Kaldellis found that imperial rhetoric about accountability and working on subjects' behalf was largely matched by actual behavior, and this alignment (unlike, he argues, much modern US foreign policy rhetoric) helped secure genuine popular consensus for centuries.
